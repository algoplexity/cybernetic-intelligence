### **Primary Hypothesis: The Algorithmic Complexity Hypothesis**

The collective, cross-sectional state of a multivariate financial system contains significantly more information about underlying market regimes than the price trajectory of any single component asset. Therefore, a structural break detector operating on a time series of the *system's aggregate algorithmic complexity* will identify regime shifts with significantly higher accuracy and confidence than one operating on a simple univariate price series.

This primary hypothesis can be broken down into three specific, falsifiable sub-hypotheses:

**Sub-Hypothesis 1: The Signal Superiority Hypothesis**

*   **Statement:** A univariate time series, `C(t)`, generated by applying an algorithmic complexity measure (such as Block Decomposition Method) to the daily cross-sectional state vector of a stock market system, will exhibit more pronounced and statistically significant non-stationarity than the price time series of any individual stock within that system.
*   **How to Test / Falsify:**
    *   Generate the `C(t)` series and the individual price series.
    *   Apply standard stationarity tests (e.g., Augmented Dickey-Fuller, KPSS) to all series.
    *   **Falsification Condition:** If the `C(t)` series is shown to be as stationary (or more stationary) than the majority of the individual price series, this hypothesis is falsified. Success is a significantly lower p-value on stationarity tests for `C(t)`.

**Sub-Hypothesis 2: The Detection Efficacy Hypothesis**

*   **Statement:** The Minimum Description Length (MDL) Change-Point Detector, using a simple Autoregressive (AR) model as a probe, will identify structural breaks in the `C(t)` time series with a significantly lower cost differential (Cost(H₀) - Cost(H₁)) than it does on any individual price series. A lower cost differential signifies a more confident and unambiguous detection of a break.
*   **How to Test / Falsify:**
    *   Run the identical MDL/AR detector on the `C(t)` series and on each of the individual stock price series.
    *   For each series, find the most likely change-point and record the MDL cost saving (the difference in bits between the one-model and two-model hypotheses).
    *   **Falsification Condition:** If the average MDL cost saving for the individual price series is comparable to or greater than the cost saving for the `C(t)` series, this hypothesis is falsified.

**Sub-Hypothesis 3: The External Validity Hypothesis**

*   **Statement:** The change-points identified by the MDL detector in the `C(t)` series will show a statistically significant temporal correlation with major, publicly documented macroeconomic events, monetary policy shifts, or market-wide liquidity events. This correlation will be significantly stronger than any correlation found between events and the change-points detected in individual price series.
*   **How to Test / Falsify:**
    *   Create a timeline of major, pre-defined market events for the period of study (e.g., FOMC interest rate decisions, major geopolitical events, dates of known flash crashes).
    *   Calculate the temporal distance between the algorithmically detected change-points and these known event dates.
    *   Perform a statistical test (e.g., a permutation test) to determine if the proximity of detected points to real events is greater than would be expected by random chance.
    *   **Falsification Condition:** If the change-points in `C(t)` do not cluster around major events with any more significance than random chance, or if they are no more correlated than the change-points from individual stocks, this hypothesis is falsified.

---

Together, these three hypotheses form a complete and rigorous foundation. They are precise, directly testable, and falsifiable. Sub-Hypothesis 1 establishes that you have created a better signal. Sub-Hypothesis 2 proves that this better signal leads to better algorithmic detection. Sub-Hypothesis 3 confirms that these detections are not just mathematical curiosities but correspond to meaningful events in the real world. Falsifying any one of them would force a significant re-evaluation, which is the mark of a strong experimental design.
