An Introduction to the QCEA Framework: Assumptions and Strategic Laws
Introduction: Why a New Way to Think About Strategy?
The Quantum-Complex-Entropic-Adaptive (QCEA) framework represents a paradigm shift in strategic thought, with the revolutionary ambition of establishing a testable science of strategy where one did not previously exist. Its goal is to provide a unified, domain-general, and falsifiable theory that moves the discipline from a collection of metaphors to a set of causal mechanisms. The framework is built in layers: a core worldview (QCEA Paradigm), a formal mathematical model (QCEA Theory), and a set of testable rules that emerge from them—the Strategic Laws. This approach directly confronts a persistent reality: most strategic initiatives fail. Large-scale studies consistently report systemic failure rates around 70% (pfail ≈ 0.70), suggesting the problem is not merely poor "execution" but a fundamental misunderstanding of the strategic environment itself.
This document serves as an introduction for students beginning their study of advanced strategy. It explains the foundational building blocks of the QCEA framework: its seven core assumptions that redefine the strategic worldview and the eighteen Strategic Laws that describe the universal constraints governing any strategic system.

1. The Core Idea of QCEA: A Worldview Shift
QCEA stands for Quantum–Complex–Entropic–Adaptive. At its heart, the framework proposes a fundamental shift in how we view the world of strategy. Instead of seeing a stable, predictable landscape where a good plan leads to a known outcome, QCEA describes a dynamic, uncertain world governed by the laws of physics and information.
This worldview shift can be summarized by contrasting it with the classical view:
Classical View of Strategy
QCEA Paradigm View
The world is a stable surface where a linear plan (A → B → C) leads to a predictable outcome (D).
The future is a probabilistic superposition of possibilities that evolve on a dynamic, interacting landscape.
Strategy is a plan to be executed.
Strategy is a process of nudging probability mass across a dynamic landscape to make favorable outcomes more likely.
Strategy as an Emergent Phenomenon
In the QCEA framework, strategy is not an optional business activity; it is an inevitable physical response to the Second Law of Thermodynamics. To merely persist—to not dissolve into chaos—a system must perform constant work against entropy. In the strategic realm, this work is not mechanical; it is the perpetual cycle of inference, decision, and feedback. Strategy, therefore, is the name we give to the coherence-preserving structure that must arise for a system to survive against the constant pull of decay and randomness.
These foundational ideas are built upon a set of core assumptions that define the operating reality for any strategist.

2. The Seven Foundational Assumptions of QCEA
These seven assumptions are the philosophical and operational pillars upon which the entire QCEA framework is built. They describe the fundamental nature of information, uncertainty, and the systems that navigate them.
1. Assumption A: Bounded Information Processing
    ◦ Core Idea: Strategic systems, like organizations or even organisms, have finite limits on the information they can process.
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: Strategic attention is a finite resource, and choices must be made within a limited set of hypotheses.
2. Assumption B: Quantum Superposition of Strategic Futures
    ◦ Core Idea: Before a decision is made, multiple potential futures coexist in a state of "superposition," like a cloud of possibilities.
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: Strategic decisions are powerful actions that "collapse" possibilities into a single reality, and the order of these decisions can lead to vastly different outcomes.
3. Assumption C: Complex-Adaptive Architecture
    ◦ Core Idea: Strategic entities are not static; they are adaptive systems that require complete feedback loops (Information-Action Cycles) to learn and respond.
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: An organization's structure must enable it to constantly sense, learn from, and adapt to its environment.
4. Assumption D: Bayesian Inferential Updating
    ◦ Core Idea: Strategic knowledge is built by rationally updating beliefs as new evidence becomes available.
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: Strategy is a process of continuous learning, where new information should systematically refine and improve the understanding of the strategic landscape.
5. Assumption E: Selective Pressure and Coherence Maintenance
    ◦ Core Idea: The environment exerts constant "selection pressure," and survival depends on maintaining internal alignment or "coherence."
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: Strategic alignment is not a one-time project; it's a constant battle against entropic drift that directly links to the organization's viability.
6. Assumption F: Dancing Landscape Dynamics
    ◦ Core Idea: The strategic environment is not a static playing field but a "dancing landscape" that is constantly reshaped by the actions of all agents and by external drift.
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: A strategist can never fully map the terrain; instead, they must focus on adapting to a constantly evolving set of opportunities and risks.
7. Assumption G: Coherence Amplification Through Networks
    ◦ Core Idea: The effects of strategic alignment can be amplified through a system's internal networks, allowing collective action to achieve more than the sum of individual parts.
    ◦ Key Implication for a Strategist: The structure of an organization's internal communication and influence networks is a critical factor in its ability to execute strategy effectively.
These seven assumptions are not abstract philosophical statements; they are the axioms that define the strategic environment. From these axioms, a series of inescapable, testable constraints—the Strategic Laws—can be derived. They describe not what a strategist should do, but what the universe permits them to do.

4. Conclusion: From Metaphor to Mechanism
The primary contribution of the QCEA framework is its ambition to transform strategy from a collection of metaphors and case studies into a unified, testable science. By grounding strategic behavior in the fundamental constraints of thermodynamics, information theory, and complexity, it provides a causal grammar for explaining why strategy emerges, what it is, and how it works. It moves the discipline from describing what successful companies have done to defining the universal mechanisms that govern success and failure for any strategic system.
For a new student of strategy, the QCEA framework offers several powerful takeaways:
• Strategy is a continuous, adaptive process, not a static plan. Success is not about having the perfect plan from the start. It is about building an effective Information-Action Cycle that allows for constant learning, updating, and adaptation in the face of new information.
• Coherence is a critical, budgetable resource. The internal alignment of an organization—its shared beliefs, norms, and goals—is not an abstract ideal. It is a measurable asset that must be actively cultivated and protected against the constant force of entropy, meaning that time, attention, and capital must be explicitly allocated to building and maintaining alignment, just as with any other critical resource.
• Uncertainty is not a bug, but a feature. The purpose of strategy is not to create a perfectly predictable world. The world is, by its nature, uncertain. The true work of strategy is to navigate that uncertainty effectively, turning probabilistic futures into advantageous realities.
